Your First Pitch: Make People Understand Your Idea

Explain the problem, show the value, and give your audience a clear reason to care.

E-Cell Blog Series Student Innovation Learning & Growth
Problem · Value · Next Step A clear idea deserves a clear pitch.

You may understand your idea perfectly and still struggle to explain it. Your audience has not followed every conversation, experiment, and decision that brought you here. A good first pitch helps people understand the problem, your proposed solution, and what you need next.

Pitching is a communication skill you can practise. You do not need complicated language or dramatic promises. You need a clear structure, relevant evidence, and an honest account of what you have built or hope to test.

1. Understand Who You Are Speaking To

A potential customer wants to know how your offer helps them. A competition judge may examine feasibility and differentiation. A mentor may need to understand your assumptions before suggesting a next step.

Keep the core idea consistent, but choose details that answer your audience’s questions. Check any presentation requirements and make your request appropriate to the setting. Do not assume every pitch needs to ask for investment.

2. Open With a Specific Problem

Identify who experiences the problem and what makes it difficult. “Students need better services” is broad. “Students looking for used course books struggle to find available copies because listings are scattered across different groups” gives the audience something concrete to understand.

You can begin with a short example, but distinguish a hypothetical scenario from a real experience. If you use a customer’s story, protect their privacy and get permission where appropriate.

Make the problem clear enough that your audience can understand why the solution matters.

3. Explain the Solution in Plain Language

Describe what your product or service does and how someone uses it. Avoid listing every feature. Lead with the action that solves the problem.

For a used-book directory, you might say: “Students can list available books and search for the course books they need in one organised directory.” Then explain how buyers contact sellers and how availability is kept accurate.

A simple screen, sample, or demonstration can help, but it should support the explanation. Make clear whether you are showing a concept, a prototype, or a working service.

4. Show Why Someone Would Choose It

Your idea will compete with what people already do, even if they currently use a group chat, a spreadsheet, or a manual process. Explain what your approach improves and why that improvement matters to the user.

Be specific without exaggerating. A claim such as “organises listings by course and shows availability” is clearer than “revolutionises student life.” Claims about savings or performance need evidence from an appropriate test.

Briefly explain how delivery could work and who might pay. If the business model is still an assumption, say so. A clear unanswered question is more credible than an invented answer.

5. Use Evidence You Actually Have

Describe what you have learned from user conversations, a prototype, or a small trial. Explain what the evidence supports and what it does not establish.

For example, interest in a demo does not prove willingness to pay. A completed purchase is useful evidence, but one purchase does not establish a large market. Give the audience enough context to interpret your results.

If you have not tested the idea yet, explain your planned experiment and what you want to learn. Avoid presenting forecasts as actual revenue or invented figures as research.

6. Build a Clear Pitch Structure

Use the following outline as a guide. Adjust it to the requirements of your audience rather than treating it as a script you must follow word for word.

The problem and the people

Who experiences the issue? What happens, and why does it matter?

The solution and its value

What are you proposing? How does it work, and why would someone choose it?

The evidence and practical plan

What have you learned? What remains uncertain? How could the solution be delivered?

The team and the request

Why is your team suited to the next step? Are you seeking feedback, a trial partner, introductions, or another specific form of support?

End with a request the listener can act on. “We are looking for a student club willing to test the prototype” gives clearer direction than “Please support our idea.”

7. Practise for Understanding, Not Just Delivery

Try your pitch with someone unfamiliar with the project. Ask them to explain the idea back to you. If they cannot identify the user, problem, or proposed solution, revise the explanation.

Prepare for questions about alternatives, costs, feasibility, and evidence. Listen to the full question before answering. If you do not know, acknowledge the gap and explain how you would investigate it.

For Team Erroris, E-Cell pitching activities offer a chance to practise this approach: explain clearly, listen carefully, and use feedback to strengthen both the presentation and the idea behind it.

A strong first pitch gives people a clear understanding of your idea and an honest reason to consider the next step. Clarity earns attention; evidence helps build trust.

Make your idea easy to understand.

One clear problem. One useful solution. One specific request.
